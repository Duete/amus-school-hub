Item 1: Organic Acid Identity & Buffer Formulation

1. Determination of the molecular formula of HA

The general formula of a saturated monocarboxylic acid is CnH2nO2.

Given: Volume of HA vapour = 10.0 cm3; volume of oxygen initially = 70.0 cm3; volume after combustion and cooling = 50.0 cm3; volume after passing through aqueous KOH = 30.0 cm3.

Step 1: Determine the volume of carbon dioxide produced

Aqueous KOH absorbs carbon dioxide.

VCO₂ = 50.0 − 30.0
VCO₂ = 20.0 cm3

Since all gases are measured at the same temperature and pressure, their volumes are proportional to their number of moles.

VCO₂ / VHA = n / 1
n = 20.0 / 10.0
n = 2

Therefore, the molecular formula of HA is C2H4O2. Hence, HA is ethanoic acid, CH3COOH.

2. Determination of the concentration of HA

The neutralisation reaction is:

HA(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaA(aq) + H2O(l)

1000 cm3 of solution contains 0.1 moles. 20.0 cm3 of solution contains:

(0.1 × 20.0)/1000 ✓ Da = 0.002 moles

Moles of HA: According to the mole ratio, 1 mole of HA reacts with 1 mole of NaOH.

Moles of HA = 0.002 moles

25.0 cm3 of HA contains 0.002 moles. 1000 cm3 of HA contains:

(0.002 × 1000)/25 = 0.08 M ✓ Dc

1 dm3 contains 0.08 moles. 500 dm3 contains:

(0.08 × 500)/1 ✓ Da = 40 moles

Molar mass of HA: The acid was C2H4O2.

RMM = (12 × 2) + 4 + 32 = 60.0 g
Moles = Mass/RMM
Mass = Moles × RMM = 40 × 60 = 2400 g ✓ Dc

Therefore, the 500 dm3 production batch contains 40.0 moles of ethanoic acid, corresponding to 2400 g of HA.

b) Evaluate the proposed buffer formulation

Proposed buffer: [HA] = 0.100 mol dm−3; [NaA] = 0.080 mol dm−3; Ka = 1.8 × 10−5; required pH = 4.60–4.80.

The buffer consists of a weak acid, HA, and its conjugate base, A−. The ionisation equilibrium is:

HA(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + A−(aq)

The acid dissociation constant is:

Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA]
pKa = −log10Ka ✓ L
pKa = 4.74

Calculate the pH of the proposed buffer using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:

pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA])

Since NaA provides A−: [A−] = 0.080 mol dm−3 and [HA] = 0.100 mol dm−3.

pH = 4.74 + log(0.080/0.100) ✓ Da
pH = 4.74 + log(0.80)
log(0.80) = −0.0969
pH = 4.74 − 0.0969
pH = 4.64 ✓ Dc

Interpretation of the calculated pH: The company requires a pH range of 4.60–4.80. The calculated buffer pH is 4.64. Since 4.60 < 4.64 < 4.80, the calculated pH falls within the required range. The proposed buffer formulation is suitable with respect to pH.

How the buffer resists changes in pH: A buffer contains a weak acid (HA) and its conjugate base (A−). When acid (H+) is added, the conjugate base removes it:

A− + H+ → HA ✓ C&R

This prevents a large decrease in pH. When alkali (OH−) is added, the weak acid reacts with it:

HA + OH− → A− + H2O ✓ C&R

This prevents a large increase in pH. Therefore, the presence of appreciable amounts of both HA and A− allows the buffer to resist sudden changes in pH.

Application in fruit processing: The buffer can be used to maintain the fruit product within the desired pH range of 4.60–4.80 despite small amounts of acid or alkali entering during processing or storage. ✓ Ea This helps maintain consistent taste, product stability and shelf life.

Item 3: Solubility Equilibria & Solvent Extraction
(a) Ionic product and solubility product

The dissolution equilibrium of calcium carbonate is:

CaCO3(s) ⇌ Ca2+(aq) + CO32−(aq)

Sample A:

Ionic product = [Ca2+][CO32−]
= (1.0 × 10−3)(2.0 × 10−7)
= 2 × 10−10 mol2 dm−6 ✓ Dc

Sample B:

Ionic product = (2.5 × 10−3)(3.0 × 10−6)
= 7.5 × 10−9 mol2 dm−6

Sample C:

Ionic product = (4.0 × 10−3)(5.0 × 10−6)
= 2 × 10−8 mol2 dm−6

Sample B and sample C are more likely to form visible deposits since their ionic product values are greater than the solubility product. This means that their solutions are supersaturated, and therefore the excess ions combine to precipitate calcium carbonate.

(b) Solvent extraction

Let the mass of Q extracted be y.

KD = 4.0 = (y/25)/((0.8 − y)/100)
4 = 100y/[25(0.8 − y)]
4 = 4y/(0.8 − y)
4(0.8 − y) = 4y
3.2 − 4y = 4y
8y = 3.2
y = 0.4 g ✓ Dc

Mass remaining in the solution = 0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4 g ✓ Dc

For the second successive extraction:

4 = (y/25)/((0.4 − y)/100)
4 = 4y/(0.4 − y)
4(0.4 − y) = 4y
1.6 − 4y = 4y
8y = 1.6
y = 0.2 g ✓ Dc

Total mass extracted = 0.4 + 0.2 = 0.6 g. Percentage of Q extracted:

Percentage extracted = (0.6 × 100)/0.8 = 75% ✓ Dc

The purification method used is effective because it extracts a higher percentage of the contaminant Q than what remains in the water.

(c) Factors affecting precipitation of calcium carbonate
  • The water picked from underground may at times contain other dissolved salts having at least one ion similar to the ions from calcium carbonate. This would increase the concentration of the ions so that the ionic product of calcium and carbonate ions would exceed the solubility product of calcium carbonate. Hence, the excess ions would react to precipitate out calcium carbonate in such water samples so as to restore equilibrium and the solubility product value of calcium carbonate.
  • Some water samples picked from underground might contain compounds that form complexes with calcium ions (complexing agents). This would reduce the concentration of the calcium ions which are free in the water and therefore more of the calcium carbonate present in water would dissolve. No precipitation would therefore occur since the salt would be becoming more soluble.
  • In water samples stored under high temperatures, precipitation cannot easily occur. This is because the solubility product of calcium carbonate is high at high temperatures and equilibrium requires high concentrations of calcium and carbonate ions to be established, which are not present in the water sample. Therefore, the ionic product of calcium and carbonate ions will be less than the solubility product value of calcium carbonate, meaning the solution will be dilute at that temperature; hence, no precipitation occurs.
Applications of ionic equilibria
  • Manufacture of soap: Ionic equilibria can be applied in the manufacture of soap. A concentrated solution of sodium chloride is added to the soap solution and this greatly increases the concentration of sodium ions since soap also possesses sodium ions. The ionic product therefore exceeds the solubility product value of soap, so that the excess sodium ions combine with carboxylate ions to precipitate out soap from its solution. ✓ Ea
  • Manufacture of steel: The knowledge of eutectic mixtures from physical equilibria can be used to make steel, an alloy of carbon and iron. A liquid mixture containing carbon and iron is cooled until the whole liquid mixture solidifies at a constant temperature to form solid carbon and solid iron, which is steel. ✓ Ea

Item 4: Graphite, Silicon Dioxide & Sodium Oxide
(a) Structure and properties

Graphite: Graphite has a giant covalent layered structure in which each carbon atom is strongly covalently bonded to three other carbon atoms. It has a very high sublimation temperature because a large amount of energy is required to overcome the strong covalent bonds. Graphite conducts electricity in the solid state because each carbon atom has one delocalised electron that is free to move through the layers and carry electrical charge.

Silicon dioxide: Silicon dioxide has a giant three-dimensional covalent structure in which silicon and oxygen atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds. It has a high melting point because considerable energy is required to break these strong bonds. It does not conduct electricity because there are no mobile ions or delocalised electrons available to carry electrical charge.

Sodium oxide: Sodium oxide has a giant ionic structure containing positively charged sodium ions and negatively charged oxide ions held together by strong electrostatic forces. It has a relatively high melting point because considerable energy is needed to overcome these attractions. It does not conduct electricity when solid because the ions are fixed in position, but it conducts when molten because the ions become free to move and carry charge.

(b) Chemical behaviour during processing and cleaning

Graphite with dilute hydrochloric acid: Graphite does not react with dilute hydrochloric acid under ordinary conditions because graphite has a strong giant covalent structure and is relatively chemically unreactive. Graphite also does not react with water under ordinary conditions because its strong carbon–carbon covalent bonds are not readily broken by water.

Silicon dioxide with dilute hydrochloric acid: Silicon dioxide is an acidic oxide and therefore does not normally react with another acid. Silicon dioxide also does not react appreciably with water because its giant covalent structure is very stable and its silicon–oxygen bonds are not easily broken by water.

However, silicon dioxide reacts with hot concentrated sodium hydroxide because silicon dioxide is an acidic oxide whereas sodium hydroxide is an alkali. They therefore undergo an acid–base reaction to form sodium silicate and water:

SiO2(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2SiO3(aq) + H2O(l) ✓ Eq

Sodium oxide with water: Sodium oxide reacts vigorously with water because it is a basic ionic oxide. The oxide ions react with hydrogen ions from water to produce hydroxide ions, forming sodium hydroxide:

Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) ✓ Eq

Sodium oxide with dilute hydrochloric acid: Sodium oxide reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid because sodium oxide is a basic oxide while hydrochloric acid is an acid. The oxide ions react with hydrogen ions to form water, while sodium ions and chloride ions remain as sodium chloride. ✓ Fe

Na2O(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) ✓ Eq
(c) Applications, health and environmental implications
  • Graphite: Graphite is suitable for electrodes because it conducts electricity and for high-temperature equipment because of its very high sublimation temperature. ✓ Ea Fine graphite dust may irritate the eyes and respiratory system when inhaled. The company should therefore control dust using good ventilation, dust extraction and appropriate protective equipment. ✓ Em
  • Silicon dioxide: Silicon dioxide is useful in the manufacture of glass, ceramics, concrete and refractory materials because of its high melting point and chemical stability. ✓ Ea However, prolonged inhalation of fine crystalline silica dust can cause serious lung disease such as silicosis. ✓ Ed The company should therefore use enclosed processes, effective ventilation, dust-suppression methods and appropriate respiratory protection. ✓ Em
  • Sodium oxide: Sodium oxide is mainly useful in the manufacture of glass and ceramics. ✓ Ea It is hazardous because it reacts with moisture to form corrosive sodium hydroxide. ✓ Ed If released into water or moist soil, it can raise the pH and harm aquatic organisms. It should therefore be stored in tightly sealed, moisture-proof containers and handled using suitable gloves, goggles and protective clothing. ✓ Em
Item 2: Aluminium Oxide, Chlorine & Carbon Dioxide
(a) Structure and physical properties

Aluminium oxide, Al2O3: Aluminium oxide has a giant ionic lattice containing aluminium ions and oxide ions held together by strong electrostatic attractions. It is a solid at room temperature and has a very high melting point of 2072 °C because a large amount of energy is required to overcome the strong attractions between the oppositely charged ions. In the solid state, the ions are fixed in position, so aluminium oxide does not conduct electricity as a solid, although it conducts when molten because the ions become mobile.

Chlorine, Cl2: Chlorine has a simple molecular structure in which two chlorine atoms are joined by a strong covalent bond. However, the chlorine molecules are held together by weak London dispersion forces. Therefore, only a small amount of energy is required to separate the molecules, giving chlorine a very low melting point of −101.5 °C. It is consequently a gas at room temperature. Chlorine does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile ions or delocalised electrons.

Carbon dioxide, CO2: Carbon dioxide has a simple molecular structure in which each carbon atom is covalently bonded to two oxygen atoms. The carbon dioxide molecules are held together by weak intermolecular forces. Therefore, little energy is required to separate the molecules, giving carbon dioxide a very low sublimation temperature of −78.5 °C. It is consequently a gas at room temperature and does not conduct electricity because it contains no mobile ions or delocalised electrons.

(b) Chemical behaviour with water and sodium hydroxide

Aluminium oxide: Aluminium oxide does not react appreciably with water under ordinary conditions because it has a stable giant ionic structure and is essentially insoluble in water. Aluminium oxide reacts with sodium hydroxide because it is amphoteric, meaning that it can react with both acids and bases. In the presence of sodium hydroxide, aluminium oxide reacts with hydroxide ions to form a soluble hydroxoaluminate compound:

Al2O3(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 3H2O(l) → 2Na[Al(OH)4](aq) ✓ Eq

Chlorine: Chlorine reacts with water because it undergoes disproportionation, whereby some chlorine is reduced while some is oxidised. The reaction produces hydrochloric acid and hypochlorous acid:

Cl2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq) ✓ Eq

With cold, dilute sodium hydroxide, chlorine undergoes disproportionation to form sodium chloride and sodium hypochlorite:

Cl2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H2O(l) ✓ Eq

With hot, concentrated sodium hydroxide, chlorine forms sodium chloride and sodium chlorate:

3Cl2(g) + 6NaOH(aq) → 5NaCl(aq) + NaClO3(aq) + 3H2O(l) ✓ Eq

Carbon dioxide: Carbon dioxide reacts with water to form carbonic acid because carbon dioxide is an acidic oxide. The reaction is reversible because carbonic acid is a weak acid:

CO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2CO3(aq) ✓ L

The carbonic acid formed partially ionises in water. This explains why water containing dissolved carbon dioxide becomes weakly acidic:

H2CO3(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq) ✓ Eq

Carbon dioxide reacts with sodium hydroxide because carbon dioxide is an acidic oxide while sodium hydroxide is an alkali. When sufficient sodium hydroxide is present, sodium carbonate and water are formed:

CO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2CO3(aq) + H2O(l) ✓ Eq

When carbon dioxide is in excess or sodium hydroxide is limited, sodium hydrogen carbonate can be formed:

CO2(g) + NaOH(aq) → NaHCO3(aq) ✓ Eq
(c) Applications, health and environmental implications, and mitigation measures
  • Aluminium oxide: Used in abrasives, ceramics, furnace linings and aluminium extraction because it is hard and has a very high melting point. ✓ Ea Fine aluminium oxide dust can irritate the eyes and respiratory system. ✓ Ed Workers should use dust masks or N95 respirators, safety goggles and gloves, while good ventilation and dust extraction should be provided. ✓ Em Waste should be collected in sealed containers to prevent dust pollution. ✓ Em
  • Chlorine: Used for water purification, swimming-pool disinfection and manufacture of bleach because it forms hypochlorous acid, which kills microorganisms. ✓ Ea Chlorine gas is toxic when inhaled and can cause serious respiratory injury. ✓ Ed It should therefore be stored in secure, cool and well-ventilated areas, with chlorine gas detectors, mechanical ventilation and leak-control systems. ✓ Em Workers should use suitable protective clothing and respiratory protection. ✓ Em Chlorinated wastewater should be dechlorinated before discharge to protect aquatic life. ✓ Em
  • Carbon dioxide: Used in carbonated drinks, fire extinguishers, food preservation and refrigeration. ✓ Ea High concentrations can displace oxygen and cause suffocation, especially in enclosed spaces. ✓ Ed Storage areas should have good ventilation and carbon dioxide detectors with alarms, while cylinders should be secured and kept away from excessive heat. ✓ Em Industries should reduce emissions through leak prevention, energy efficiency and carbon dioxide recovery or capture where practical. ✓ Em
Item 3: Halogens & Group II Carbonates
1. Physical properties of the halogens

The halogens are non-metallic elements found in Group VII of the periodic table. Fluorine and chlorine are gases at room temperature, bromine is a reddish-brown liquid, while iodine is a dark grey solid. ✓ Pe Down the group, the atomic size and number of electrons increase, resulting in stronger London dispersion forces between the molecules. ✓ Fe Therefore, the melting and boiling points generally increase down the group.

The halogens exist as diatomic molecules, for example fluorine exists as F2 and chlorine as Cl2. They are poor conductors of electricity because they contain no mobile charged particles.

The bond energy generally decreases from chlorine to iodine because the atoms become larger and the bonding electrons are further from the nuclei. ✓ Pe The fluorine–fluorine bond is unusually weak because the small fluorine atoms have strong electron–electron repulsion between their bonding electrons. ✓ Pe Therefore, the chlorine–chlorine bond has the highest bond energy among the four given halogens and requires more energy to break than the bromine–bromine and iodine–iodine bonds.

2. Physical properties of Group II carbonates

Group II carbonates are ionic compounds consisting of Group II metal ions and carbonate ions. They are generally white or coloured crystalline solids with high melting points because strong electrostatic attractions exist between the oppositely charged ions. ✓ Pe

Their solubility generally increases down Group II. Magnesium carbonate is sparingly soluble, while the carbonates of calcium, strontium and barium become progressively more soluble. The change in solubility occurs because, down the group, the attraction between the larger metal ions and carbonate ions becomes weaker, making it easier for the ionic lattice to separate and dissolve. ✓ Fe

Reactions of halogens with water

Chlorine and water: Chlorine reacts reversibly with water because it undergoes disproportionation, where some chlorine is reduced to chloride while some is oxidised to chlorine in hypochlorous acid. ✓ Fe

Cl2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HClO(aq) ✓ Eq

Bromine and water: Bromine also reacts reversibly with water by disproportionation, producing hydrobromic acid and hypobromous acid:

Br2(l) + H2O(l) ⇌ HBr(aq) + HBrO(aq)

Iodine and water: Iodine reacts only very slightly with water because it is less reactive and its iodine–iodine bond is relatively weak but iodine has a lower tendency to undergo the reaction with water under these conditions. The equilibrium lies strongly towards iodine and water, so very little reaction occurs:

I2(s) + H2O(l) ⇌ HI(aq) + HIO(aq)

Fluorine and water: Fluorine reacts very vigorously with water because fluorine is the most electronegative and strongest oxidising halogen. It oxidises water, producing hydrogen fluoride and oxygen:

2F2(g) + 2H2O(l) → 4HF(aq) + O2(g) ✓ Eq
Behaviour of halogens with alkalis

Chlorine with cold dilute sodium hydroxide: Chlorine reacts with cold dilute sodium hydroxide by disproportionation. Chlorine is simultaneously reduced to chloride and oxidised to hypochlorite:

Cl2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H2O(l)

Chlorine with hot concentrated sodium hydroxide: When hot concentrated sodium hydroxide is used, chlorine undergoes further disproportionation to form sodium chloride and sodium chlorate:

3Cl2(g) + 6NaOH(aq) → 5NaCl(aq) + NaClO3(aq) + 3H2O(l) ✓ Eq
3. Solubility of Group II carbonates in hydrochloric acid

Group II carbonates react with hydrochloric acid because carbonates contain carbonate ions, which react with hydrogen ions from the acid to form carbon dioxide and water. ✓ Fe

For example, magnesium carbonate reacts with hydrochloric acid as follows:

MgCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g) ✓ Eq

Calcium carbonate reacts similarly:

CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)

The reaction occurs because hydrogen ions react with carbonate ions. Carbonic acid is initially formed but rapidly decomposes into carbon dioxide and water:

CO32−(aq) + 2H+(aq) → H2O(l) + CO2(g)

The visible effervescence observed is therefore due to the formation of carbon dioxide gas.

(c) Application of chlorine as a cleaning agent

Chlorine is used in water treatment, swimming pools and cleaning agents because it reacts with water to form hypochlorous acid, which is a strong oxidising agent that kills microorganisms. ✓ Ea

However, chlorine is toxic and irritating when present in high concentrations, and excessive chlorine discharged into water can harm aquatic organisms. ✓ Ed Therefore, chlorine products should be used at the recommended concentration, stored in closed containers and handled in well-ventilated areas. ✓ Em Workers should wear chemical-resistant gloves and safety goggles. ✓ Em

Item 6: Chlorine-Containing Compounds in Water Treatment
(a) Physical properties and structures

Sodium chloride: Sodium chloride is a solid with high melting and boiling points. ✓ Pe Sodium chloride has a giant ionic structure with strong ionic bonds between the sodium and chloride ions in the lattice, making it a solid. The ionic bonds require a lot of heat energy to break and hence the high melting point.

Aluminium chloride: Aluminium chloride is a solid, however, with low melting and boiling points. ✓ Pe Aluminium chloride is supposed to be ionic, but due to the small ionic radius and high ionic charge, the aluminium ion has a high charge density and high polarising power and hence greatly polarises the chloride ions, making it covalent partly. Its molecules interact via weak van der Waals forces, which are, however, strong enough to make it a solid but can easily be broken under low amounts of heat.

Tin(IV) chloride: Tin(IV) chloride has a simple molecular structure, as well as chlorine. Their molecules interact via weak van der Waals forces of attraction and hence require less heat energy to break, hence low melting point.

Tin(IV) chloride, however, has a higher molecular mass and hence stronger van der Waals forces of attraction. This makes it a liquid since its molecules are more closely and strongly attracted to each other and hence has a higher melting point/boiling point than chlorine.

Reactions with water

Sodium chloride dissociates in water to form sodium ions and chloride ions. This is because it is a salt formed from a strong acid and strong base and hence it does not undergo hydrolysis:

NaCl(aq) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq) ✓ Eq

Aluminium chloride undergoes hydrolysis in water to form aluminium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid. This is due to the small ionic radius and high charge, hence high charge density and high polarising power. It greatly polarises the water molecules to release hydrogen ions:

Al3+(aq) + 3H2O(l) ⇌ Al(OH)3(s) + 3H+(aq) ✓ Eq

Tin(IV) chloride undergoes hydrolysis to form tin(IV) oxide and hydrogen chloride. This is because tin has a vacant d-orbital which accommodates a lone pair of electrons from the oxygen atom of water to form coordinate bonds, and hence the reaction:

SnCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) → SnO2(s) + 4HCl(aq) ✓ Eq

Chlorine disproportionates in water to form hydrochloric acid and hypochlorous acid:

Cl2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCl(aq) + HOCl(aq) ✓ Eq

Aluminium chloride hydrolyses to form an acidic solution and hence reacts with cold dilute sodium hydroxide to form aluminium hydroxide and sodium chloride:

AlCl3(aq) + 3NaOH(aq) → Al(OH)3(s) + 3NaCl(aq) ✓ Eq

Chlorine disproportionates in cold dilute sodium hydroxide solution to form sodium chloride, sodium chlorate(I) and water:

Cl2(aq) + 2OH−(aq) → Cl−(aq) + ClO−(aq) + H2O(l) ✓ Eq

or

Cl2(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + NaOCl(aq) + H2O(l)
(b) Applications of chlorine in water treatment
  • Chlorine dissolves in water to form hypochlorous acid and hydrochloric acid. ✓ Ea This lowers the pH, making it unfavourable for multiplication of microorganisms, and hypochlorous acid also destroys bacterial cell walls and oxidises their nucleic acids, preventing them from reproducing.
  • Chlorine, being a strong oxidising agent, oxidises compounds produced by algae and bacteria that would have caused unpleasant smell and taste.
  • Chlorine also oxidises dissolved substances such as iron(II) ions and manganese(II) ions, and these can hence be easily removed during filtration.

Danger:

  • Long-term exposure to high concentrations of chlorine causes cancers like bladder cancer, liver damage and kidney damage. ✓ Ed
  • Too much chlorine used in water at times causes irritation of the eyes and the respiratory system, resulting in severe coughing and difficulty in breathing. ✓ Ed
  • High chlorine levels/concentrations corrode metal pipes, pumps and water tanks, and this shortens the equipment lifespan and releases metals into water, making it unsafe for human consumption/use. ✓ Ed
Mitigation
  • Use alternative water treatment methods like coagulation, filtration or sedimentation, which do not use poisonous chemicals while greatly improving water quality and hence avoiding diseases associated with chlorine. ✓ Em
  • The amount of chlorine must be checked and controlled regularly to ensure that the appropriate levels are used in water before its supply for human consumption, and pH must be maintained in the optimum range (6.5–7.5). ✓ Em
Item 7: Period 3 Oxides — Industrial & Environmental Implications
(a) Structures and trends

Sulphur trioxide has a simple molecular structure in which its molecules interact via weak van der Waals forces of attraction, which are easily broken under low heat energy, and hence it easily fumes since its particles are far apart, making it a gas. ✓ Pe

Sodium oxide, magnesium oxide and aluminium oxide have giant ionic structures with strong ionic bonds between the metal ions and the oxide ions, which require high amounts of heat to break. ✓ Pe From sodium oxide to magnesium oxide, the heat required increases due to increase in magnitude of charge of the cation, hence forming stronger ionic bonds. ✓ Fe

Aluminium oxide has relatively lower heat energy required/melting point due to the small ionic radius and high charge of the aluminium ion, which gives it a high charge density and high polarising power and hence greatly tends to polarise the oxide ion, reducing the ionic character. ✓ Fe

Silicon(IV) oxide has a giant covalent/molecular structure with strong covalent bonds between the silicon and oxygen atoms, which are continuously bonded to each other. ✓ Pe The bonds hence require a lot of heat to break, giving it a high melting point.

(b) Reactions

Sodium oxide is highly ionic and hence reacts with cold water vigorously to form sodium hydroxide:

Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) ✓ Eq

Magnesium oxide is also ionic, but the magnesium ion has a higher charge density, giving it a high lattice energy, hence dissolves slowly in cold water to form magnesium hydroxide:

MgO(s) + H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(aq) ✓ Eq

Sulphur trioxide is strongly acidic and polar, hence highly reactive with cold water to form sulphuric acid:

SO3(g) + H2O(l) → H2SO4(aq) ✓ Eq

Aluminium oxide, being amphoteric, reacts with hot concentrated sodium hydroxide to form sodium aluminate:

Al2O3(s) + 2OH−(aq) + 3H2O(l) → 2Al(OH)4−(aq) ✓ Eq

Sulphur trioxide, being acidic in nature, reacts with cold dilute sodium hydroxide to form sodium sulphate and water. ✓ Fe

SO3(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + H2O(l) ✓ Eq

Item 1: Organic Conversions — Boiling Points, Mechanisms & Synthesis

Ethene has the lowest boiling point because it is a small, non-polar molecule. ✓ Pe Its molecules are held together only by weak London dispersion forces. ✓ Pe Therefore, little energy is required to overcome the intermolecular forces and separate the molecules.

Ethanol has a much higher boiling point because it contains an –OH group. The OH groups of neighbouring ethanol molecules form hydrogen bonds, which are stronger than London forces. More energy is therefore required to separate the molecules.

Propan-2-ol also forms hydrogen bonds because it contains an –OH group. It has a slightly larger molecular size than ethanol, giving it stronger London dispersion forces in addition to hydrogen bonding. Consequently, its boiling point is slightly higher than that of ethanol.

Propanoic acid has the highest boiling point because its –COOH groups form strong hydrogen bonds. Two carboxylic acid molecules can form hydrogen-bonded dimers. A large amount of energy is therefore required to separate the molecules.

Water is a polar solvent, and substances that can form strong interactions, especially hydrogen bonds, with water tend to be more soluble.

  • Ethene is only slightly soluble in water because it is non-polar and cannot form hydrogen bonds with water.
  • Ethanol is completely miscible with water because its polar –OH group forms hydrogen bonds with water molecules.
  • Propan-2-ol is also highly soluble/miscible in water because its OH group forms hydrogen bonds with water. However, its larger hydrocarbon portion makes the molecule somewhat more non-polar than ethanol.
  • Propanoic acid is highly soluble in water because its COOH group forms hydrogen bonds with water. It also partially ionises in water.
(b) Organic reaction mechanisms

Item 1: Ammonia Ionisation — Enthalpy Determination

Ammonia solution is used in the manufacture of household and industrial cleaning products. ✓ Ea Its ionisation in water affects the concentration of hydroxide ions produced.

Provided reagents:

  • FA1: 30 cm3 of 2.0 M ammonia solution
  • FA2: 30 cm3 of 2.0 M sodium hydroxide solution
  • FA3: 100 cm3 of 2.0 M hydrochloric acid

Specific heat capacity = 4.2 J g−1 K−1; density of solutions = 1.0 g cm−3.

Aim

An investigation to determine the enthalpy of ionisation of ammonia by adding increasing volumes of hydrochloric acid to fixed volumes of ammonia solution and sodium hydroxide solution separately and measuring the maximum temperature attained after each addition in order to assess the quality and suitability of the ammonia sample for use in cleaning products.

Hypothesis

The enthalpy of ionisation of the ammonia sample obtained by adding increasing volumes of hydrochloric acid to fixed volumes of ammonia solution and sodium hydroxide solution separately and measuring the maximum temperature attained after each addition will lie within the reference range of +4.5 to +8.0 kJ mol−1, indicating that the sample is suitable for use in cleaning products.

Variables
  • Independent variable: Volume of FA3 added / nature of alkali used
  • Dependent variable: Maximum temperature attained by the reaction mixture
  • Controlled variables: Volume of the alkalis used was fixed at 25.0 cm3 for each
Procedure
  • (a) 25.0 cm3 of FA1 was measured using a pipette/measuring cylinder and transferred into a clean polystyrene cup.
  • (b) The initial temperature of FA1 was measured using a thermometer and recorded.
  • (c) 5.0 cm3 portions of FA3 were measured and added to FA1. The mixture was stirred and the maximum temperature attained was recorded after every addition.
  • (d) Procedures (a) to (c) were repeated using fresh 25.0 cm3 portions of FA2 instead of FA1 under identical conditions.
  • (e) The results were tabulated.

  • Zinc ions (Zn2+): Essential in trace amounts but elevated concentrations may impart an undesirable metallic taste and reduce the suitability of water for domestic use. ✓ Ed

Community action / mitigation: The community should reduce runoff from the nearby construction site by improving drainage and erosion control measures, protect water sources from contamination, and treat the water through sedimentation, filtration and, where necessary, water softening before domestic use. ✓ Em
